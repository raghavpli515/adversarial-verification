# Apollo Guidance Computer

The Apollo Guidance Computer (AGC) was the onboard flight computer used in both the Command Module and the Lunar Module during Apollo missions. It was designed and built by the MIT Instrumentation Laboratory, led by Charles Stark Draper, and manufactured by Raytheon.

The AGC was one of the first computers to be built using integrated circuits rather than discrete transistors, and its adoption was a major early driver of demand for integrated-circuit manufacturing in the 1960s. It used a form of read-only memory called core rope memory, in which software was physically woven into the wiring during manufacturing, making it extremely reliable but impossible to modify after production. Astronauts interacted with the AGC through a numeric interface called the DSKY (display and keyboard), entering commands as two-digit "verb" and "noun" codes.

During the Apollo 11 landing, the AGC triggered a series of program alarms, most notably "1202" and "1201" alarms, caused by the computer being overloaded with more tasks than it could process in real time, partly because a rendezvous radar switch had been left in the wrong position. Mission Control, aided by the computer's design that prioritized landing-critical tasks and discarded lower-priority ones, judged the alarms non-fatal and the landing proceeded successfully.

The AGC's software was developed under the direction of Margaret Hamilton, who led the Software Engineering Division at MIT's Instrumentation Laboratory and is widely credited with helping establish software engineering as a rigorous discipline.
