# Apollo Program Overview

The Apollo program was the United States human spaceflight program carried out by NASA between 1961 and 1972, with the goal of landing astronauts on the Moon and returning them safely to Earth. It was announced by President John F. Kennedy in a May 1961 speech to Congress and reaffirmed in his September 1962 "We choose to go to the Moon" address at Rice University.

Apollo followed the single-astronaut Mercury program and the two-astronaut Gemini program, both of which developed techniques (orbital rendezvous, docking, spacewalks, long-duration flight) that Apollo depended on. The program used the three-stage Saturn V rocket to launch a Command/Service Module (CSM) and a Lunar Module (LM) toward the Moon.

Apollo flew 15 crewed missions between 1968 and 1972: Apollo 7 through Apollo 17, skipping no numbers in that range but starting the crewed missions at 7 (Apollo 1 through 6 cover the fire, uncrewed tests, and unflown numbers). Six of these missions — Apollo 11, 12, 14, 15, 16, and 17 — landed astronauts on the lunar surface. Apollo 8 and 10 orbited the Moon without landing. Apollo 9 tested the Lunar Module in Earth orbit. Apollo 13 was intended to land but aborted after an in-flight explosion.

Twelve astronauts walked on the Moon across the six landing missions. Eugene Cernan, commander of Apollo 17, was the last person to walk on the Moon, in December 1972. No human has walked on the Moon since. Three further missions, Apollo 18, 19, and 20, were planned but cancelled before launch, primarily due to budget cuts.

The program cost approximately $25.4 billion at the time (roughly $150 billion in early-2020s dollars). Hardware and funding freed by the cancellation of Apollo 18–20 was redirected to the Skylab space station program and the 1975 Apollo–Soyuz Test Project, a joint mission with the Soviet Union that used a leftover Apollo Command/Service Module.
